# Initial SRS Observations — Starhawk Mission Computer SRS

**Document reviewed:** `data/Starhawk Mission Computer SRS.md` (Doc ID SH-SRS-001, v1.0, Status: Draft for Review)
**Context:** First-read pass, pre-standards-review. These are raw engineering observations and open questions from reading the document once, straight through, without attempting to fix or formally grade anything. Verdicts (ACCEPTABLE / NEEDS REVIEW) are a later activity for someone else on the team.

---

1. **Overall structure is easy to follow.** The SRS is organized into Introduction, External Interfaces, Software Requirements (by subsystem: Navigation, Propulsion, Targeting/Weapons, Defensive, Communications, Vehicle Health), Emergency Operation, Performance, Reliability, Data, Security, Constraints, a Traceability section, and an Open Items section. Explanatory/contextual material (system overview, operational modes, interface descriptions) is consistently separated from requirements by being placed in its own numbered subsections (1.x and 2.x) before Section 3 begins the actual requirement statements.

2. **Requirements are identified with a consistent ID scheme and shall-statements.** Every requirement has a unique tag `SFMC-REQ-0##` plus a short title (e.g., "SFMC-REQ-008 — Fuel Warning"), and the requirement body almost always uses "shall" phrasing. This makes requirements easy to distinguish from prose: descriptive text (Sections 1, 2, and the mission-need table in Section 10) never uses "shall" and is never numbered with the REQ- prefix.

3. **A developer would get concrete, testable trigger conditions from many requirements** — e.g., SFMC-REQ-008/009 give exact fuel percentage thresholds (15% / 5%), SFMC-REQ-002 gives an update rate (500 ms), SFMC-REQ-036 gives a startup time (30 s), and SFMC-REQ-038 gives a display refresh rate (10 Hz). These read like they could go almost directly into unit/integration test cases. A tester would similarly get clear pass/fail conditions from these numeric ones, but would have nothing measurable to test against for the vague ones noted below.

4. **Several requirements use subjective, non-verifiable language that a developer or tester cannot act on without further definition.** Examples: SFMC-REQ-006 ("reasonably close" to destination), SFMC-REQ-016 ("extremely accurate" targeting), SFMC-REQ-021 ("respond quickly" to threats), SFMC-REQ-025 ("important" communications must be encrypted — "important" undefined), SFMC-REQ-037 ("without noticeable delay"), and SFMC-REQ-050 ("state-of-the-art security techniques"). None of these give a number, tolerance, or acceptance test a developer could implement against or a tester could verify against.

5. **Section 11 "Open Items" is essentially a confession list of the same gaps** — it explicitly flags undefined terms: "safe route" (ties to REQ-004), acceptable arrival distance (ties to REQ-006), "critical fault" (ties to REQ-030), "important communication" (ties to REQ-025), "safe location" during emergency (ties to REQ-033/034), required log storage capacity (ties to REQ-047), required targeting accuracy (ties to REQ-016), max acceptable command response time (ties to REQ-037), required cybersecurity standards (ties to REQ-050), and friend/hostile determination criteria (ties to REQ-015). This is useful — the document is self-aware about its own weak spots — but it means roughly 10 requirements are not yet independently testable as written.

6. **Absolute/superlative requirements stand out as unusually strong and may be unrealistic as written.** SFMC-REQ-040 ("The SFMC software shall never crash during a mission") and SFMC-REQ-015 ("shall never permit the pilot to fire a weapon at a friendly spacecraft") use "never," which is a very high bar with no stated verification method (how would this ever be proven true rather than just not-yet-observed-false?). By contrast, SFMC-REQ-001 ("shall display the spacecraft's current position") is comparatively weak — it has no update rate, accuracy, or format attached to it on its own (the rate is only supplied two requirements later, in REQ-002).

7. **Traceability is acknowledged as incomplete by the document itself.** Section 10 maps 10 mission needs (MN-01 through MN-10) to the requirements narratively, but the document states outright: "A complete requirement-to-mission-need traceability matrix has not yet been developed." This means we can't yet verify every REQ traces to a mission need, or that every mission need is covered by at least one REQ.

8. **Question for stakeholders — quantify the vague terms.** For each Open Items entry (especially targeting accuracy in REQ-016, response time in REQ-037, and security standard in REQ-050), what numeric tolerance, percentile, or named standard (e.g., a specific encryption standard for REQ-050/025) should replace the current subjective language, and who owns supplying that number?

9. **Question for stakeholders — "never" requirements.** For REQ-040 and REQ-015, is "never" meant literally (i.e., is this a safety-critical requirement requiring formal verification / fault-tree analysis), or is it shorthand for "shall meet a defined reliability target"? If literal, what verification approach is expected, since testing alone cannot prove a negative universally?

10. **Question for stakeholders — friend/hostile determination.** REQ-015 forbids firing on a friendly spacecraft, but Open Item #10 says the criteria for friend/hostile classification don't exist yet. Given this is a weapons-safety requirement, should classification-criteria work be prioritized ahead of other open items, and what happens (per REQ-014/015) if classification is ambiguous or a target's IFF status is unavailable?
